Why this game?

On a reversible chip, a step's energy falls the slower it runs and grows with its gates × depth, but erasing still costs a fixed amount per bit (Landauer). So the best plan keeps, uncomputes or erases each value depending on its size and its circuit.

In this model Strassen's 7-multiply method costs more than the plain 8-multiply one (best 170 against 68), because its combined steps are deeper.

Slide the hardware friction to watch the best plan change: on frictionless hardware every reversible step is free and nothing needs erasing; on very lossy hardware erasing everything wins.

Prices at standard friction: an add costs 1 and a multiply 4 (gates × depth); erasing costs the same per bit within a level.

Landauer 1961 · Bennett 1989 · Strassen 1969 · Thumann & Soloveichik, arXiv:2610.24435
